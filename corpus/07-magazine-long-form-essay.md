# Notes on Reading Slowly

A long-form essay on the discipline of reading at the pace of comprehension rather than the pace of consumption — and on what changes when you do.

## I

There is a particular kind of fatigue that arrives in the late afternoon, after a day of reading. It is not the fatigue of physical effort, nor of attention sustained beyond its capacity, but a more diffuse condition. The mind has been asked to do something it is good at but does not enjoy: to absorb, at speed, a great quantity of words none of which it cares about deeply.

The cure, prescribed at length by every essay on the subject, is to read more slowly. The advice is correct, but its correctness has been worn flat by repetition. To read more slowly is not, as many imagine, simply to take more time. It is to read with a different relationship to the text — with more skin in the game, more willingness to disagree, more patience with passages that resist easy summary.

> *Reading furnishes the mind only with materials of knowledge; it is thinking that makes what we read ours.*
>
> — John Locke

I find I do this best at three particular times: early in the morning, when the day's work has not yet asserted its claims; in the middle hours of the night, when whatever I am reading is the only voice in the house; and on long, uneventful train rides, where the rocking is soothing and the windows present nothing important.

## II

To read slowly is to allow the structure of a paragraph to register. A well-built paragraph has a shape, the way a building has rooms — a thesis, a turning point, a closing. When you read at speed you skip over the architecture and take only the headline. The headline is rarely the interesting part.

There are three habits I have adopted, gradually, over years.

1. **Read with a pencil.** Not always to underline, but to leave a small mark in the margin where something resists me, where I would push back, where I would want to copy a sentence and bring it home.
2. **Reread the previous paragraph before moving on.** This sounds tedious. It is. After a few months of practice, it becomes invisible. The compounding effect of having always read each paragraph at least twice is enormous.
3. **Stop at the boundary of comprehension.** If I do not understand what I have just read, I do not push past it. I stop, walk away from the desk, and return after some other activity has rinsed the brain clean.

These habits do not make reading faster, of course; they make it slower. The output, measured in pages per hour, falls. The input, measured in *retained insight per page*, rises by a much greater factor. I cannot prove this mathematically, but the asymmetry is dramatic enough that I do not need to.

## III

There is a useful exercise I learned from a friend who teaches literature. Choose a single page of a book you have read before — any book, but ideally one whose density rewards attention. Read the page. Then, without referring to it, write down everything you can remember, in any order. Then read the page again, and note what you missed.

The first time I tried this on a page of *Middlemarch*, I retrieved roughly a quarter of what was there. The second time, on the same page, two-thirds. The third time, eight-ninths. The page had not changed; I had begun to be a different reader of it.

Most of what we read, we forget within a week. The fraction we retain decays roughly geometrically with time elapsed since the reading. Slowing down delays the start of the curve and steepens its long tail. Both effects are large.

## IV

> *We are what we pretend to be, so we must be careful what we pretend to be.*
>
> — Kurt Vonnegut, *Mother Night*

Every book is a *pretending exercise*. For some hours, you take on the world-model of someone else — their assumptions, their grudges, their categories, their music. The longer you pretend, the more some of it stays. This is the deepest reason to be careful about what you read, and unhurried in how you read it. Books that pass through you quickly do not leave much; books you have lived inside for a week have moved a piece of your interior furniture.

There is a counterargument, made well by people whose taste I respect: you must read widely, and widely is incompatible with slowly. There is only so much time. I do not think this argument is entirely wrong, but I think it is mostly wrong. Reading thirty mediocre books in the time it would take to read three excellent ones is an act of consumption, not learning. The thirty become a haze; the three become friends.

## V

I keep a notebook. The notebook is small and not particularly elegant. Into it I copy, by hand, the sentences I want to keep. Sometimes only a phrase. Sometimes a paragraph. Once or twice, several pages from a chapter that I felt I needed to commit to muscle memory.

The act of copying has a curious effect on attention. It is not possible to copy a long sentence without slowing to its rhythm. You feel where the writer chose to break the clause, where they chose to use the passive voice, where they hesitated and then committed. The sentences you copy become available to you in conversation later, not as quotations but as moves you can make.

This is, I suspect, what people meant when they used to say that reading is a kind of training. The vocabulary shapes the thinking, but the *pace* of reading shapes how thoroughly the vocabulary is absorbed.

## VI

A short list of books that have rewarded me most for slow reading:

- *The Long Loneliness*, Dorothy Day
- *The Brothers Karamazov*, Dostoevsky (Pevear/Volokhonsky translation)
- *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek*, Annie Dillard
- *Speak, Memory*, Vladimir Nabokov
- *Notes on the Synthesis of Form*, Christopher Alexander

None of these are books one races through. All of them are books one finishes a different person.

## VII

There is a final argument for reading slowly, and it is the simplest one: you only get to read each book once, in the sense that *first impressions are not recoverable*. The second reading is informed by the memory of the first. You cannot un-meet a character or un-feel a scene. Speed is a way of throwing away your one shot.

It seems strange to me, in retrospect, that for most of my twenties I treated books as things to be defeated — counted, listed, finished. I was younger and better at being defeated by books than I knew. The books I conquered were the books that left no mark.

The shift, when it came, was not a decision so much as a giving-up. I gave up the count. I gave up the pace. I gave up the list. What I have left is fewer books, more underlines, longer sentences in my own writing, and a slightly different relationship to the time I spend in chairs.

## VIII

It is, I admit, slightly absurd to write a long essay arguing for a slower kind of reading. The reader who has read this far has already conceded the point. The reader who skimmed has skimmed a different essay, and we are not really in conversation.

To them, briefly: the next book you pick up, try the experiment. Read the first chapter, then close the book and write down what you remember. Open the book and read the chapter again. The asymmetry between what you thought you absorbed and what you actually absorbed is the thing this essay is about. There is no need to take my word for it. The book itself is the experiment.

The sentence is short, the project endless. Reading slowly is a small, daily form of it. Not the only form. But not nothing either.
