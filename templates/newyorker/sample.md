# A Cabinet of Hours

A demonstration of the New Yorker template — Hoefler Text and Big Caslon serif, narrow column, engraved drop cap, italic byline, single burgundy accent. The mood is editorial, slow, and a little bit nineteenth-century.

## Of Drop Caps

The drop cap is, in some way, the most affected of typographic gestures. It announces — *the article begins here* — and then steps aside. When the type face is well chosen and the color is restrained, the effect is something like a chime at the start of a piece of music: a small, deliberate signal that the piece has begun.

A New Yorker drop cap is set in the same family as the body, only larger, and ideally in a color picked from the accent palette. Burgundy works for serious essays; a deeper warm black works when the topic is heavier. Avoid red — it reads cheap.

## On the Justification of Lines

The body is justified, hyphenated, and set on a narrow measure — about sixty-two characters — for the simple reason that it is comfortable to read. Modern web layouts have trained us to accept lines of ninety or one hundred characters that the eye must scan from end to end without rest. In a long-form essay, this becomes exhausting in a way that few readers would name aloud.

Hyphenation is not optional. A justified line without hyphenation produces those rivers of whitespace that designers spend whole careers chasing out of the column. With hyphenation, the rhythm of the line settles down and the type face's particular handling of word-spacing becomes legible.

> *A great paragraph is a small machine for changing the reader's mind without giving the reader the impression of having been changed.*

## A Quiet Table

The New Yorker is not a magazine of tables; when one is needed, it is set in italic headers, no rules below the body, and just enough chrome to mark it as an interruption.

## A Footnote

A piece may carry a footnote when the parenthetical aside has grown too long for the line.[^1] The bottom-of-section setting is small, italic, and quiet.

[^1]: The footnote gives the writer a controlled place to digress without breaking the rhythm of the body. Use sparingly: too many footnotes are a sign that the body needs to be rewritten.

## Closing

The template tries to put the writing first and the design second, which is the right order. If a reader notices the design, the design has, in some sense, failed.
